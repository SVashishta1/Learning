The Bicycle: A Simple Machine That Changed the World

A bicycle looks simple, but it is a clever collection of small machines working together. When you push down on a pedal, the crank turns a toothed wheel called the chainring. The chain wraps around the chainring and around a smaller toothed wheel on the back wheel, called a sprocket or cog. Because the chainring has more teeth than the cog, one turn of the pedals makes the back wheel turn more than once. This is how a rider can travel much faster on a bicycle than on foot while using the same amount of effort.

Gears let the rider change this relationship. On a steep hill, the rider chooses a small chainring and a large cog. Each turn of the pedals then moves the bicycle only a short distance, but the pedals are easy to push. On a flat road with the wind behind, the rider chooses a large chainring and a small cog. The pedals become harder to push, but every turn carries the bicycle a long way. A derailleur is the part that pushes the chain sideways from one cog to the next when the rider moves the gear lever.

Brakes turn the energy of movement into heat. Rim brakes squeeze rubber pads against the edge of the wheel. Disc brakes squeeze pads against a metal disc fixed to the centre of the wheel. Disc brakes keep working well in rain and mud, which is why they are now common on mountain bikes and on many road bikes. Whatever the type, good brakes depend on clean surfaces and pads that are not worn out.

Staying upright on a bicycle seems like magic to a young child, yet almost everyone learns it. A moving bicycle is steered back under its rider without the rider thinking about it. When the bicycle starts to lean to the left, the rider turns the handlebars slightly to the left, and the wheels move back under the centre of weight. The faster the bicycle moves, the smaller these corrections need to be, which is why riding very slowly is harder than riding at a steady speed. The shape of the front fork and the angle of the steering also help the bicycle steer itself a little.

The history of the bicycle began in the early nineteenth century. In 1817 a German inventor named Karl Drais built a wooden running machine. It had two wheels in a line and a handlebar for steering, but no pedals. The rider sat on it and pushed along the ground with both feet. People called it the dandy horse. It was fun on smooth paths, but most roads at the time were rough, and the machine was soon forgotten by most people.

In the 1860s, makers in France added pedals directly to the front wheel. These machines were called velocipedes, and because they had iron tyres on wooden wheels, riders gave them the nickname boneshakers. With the pedals fixed to the front wheel, the only way to go faster was to make that wheel bigger. This led to the high wheeler of the 1870s and 1880s, also called the penny farthing, with a huge front wheel and a tiny back wheel. It was fast, but a rider who hit a stone could be thrown over the handlebars, a fall that was often painful and sometimes dangerous.

The safety bicycle solved this problem in the 1880s. It had two wheels of the same size, and the rider pushed pedals that drove the back wheel through a chain. The rider sat lower and between the wheels, so falls were rarer and less serious. A few years later, air-filled rubber tyres made the ride far more comfortable. By the 1890s, the shape of the bicycle was almost the same as the one we ride today, and a cycling craze spread across Europe and North America.

The bicycle changed daily life in ways that are easy to forget. Working people could live farther from their factories and offices. Young people could travel to nearby villages to meet friends. Many women found new freedom, because a bicycle let them travel alone, quickly and cheaply. Clothing changed to make riding easier. Roads were improved because cyclists campaigned for smoother surfaces, years before cars became common.

Many ideas that later helped build cars and aeroplanes were first worked out on bicycles. Ball bearings, light steel tubes, chain drives and air-filled tyres were all developed or improved for bicycles. The Wright brothers, who made the first powered aeroplane flights in 1903, ran a bicycle shop and used the money and skills from that business to build their machines.

Today there are more than a billion bicycles in the world. In some cities, such as Amsterdam and Copenhagen, a large share of all trips are made by bicycle. Riders choose bicycles because they are cheap to own, easy to park, good for health and quiet. A bicycle uses no fuel and produces no exhaust, so it is one of the most efficient ways for a person to travel. Electric bicycles add a small motor that helps the rider push the pedals, which makes hills and long distances easier and has brought cycling to many people who had stopped riding.

Looking after a bicycle is not difficult. The tyres should be pumped to the pressure written on their side, because soft tyres make riding slow and can lead to punctures. The chain should be cleaned and oiled now and then, so that it runs smoothly and does not rust. Brakes should be checked before every ride: squeeze each lever and make sure the wheel stops firmly. Lights and a bell make the rider easier to see and hear, and a helmet protects the head in a fall.

Learning to fix a puncture is a useful first repair. The rider removes the wheel, lifts the tyre off the rim with two plastic levers and pulls out the inner tube. Pumping a little air into the tube and listening or feeling for escaping air shows where the hole is. The area around the hole is roughened with sandpaper, a patch is pressed firmly over it, and the tube is put back. Before refitting the tyre, the rider runs a finger carefully around the inside of the tyre to find and remove the thorn or piece of glass that caused the puncture, so that it does not happen again on the next ride.

The bicycle has lasted for more than two hundred years because it does one job very well. It takes the power of a human body and turns it into movement with very little waste. It is a machine that a child can ride and an adult can repair with a few simple tools, and it still carries millions of people to school, to work and to adventure every single day.
